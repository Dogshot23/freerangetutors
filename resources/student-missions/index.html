Student Missions

Sixteen short task cards built from a student's own memory, imagination, and everyday objects — each with a clear mechanic and something real to report back. Works equally well handed out at the end of a lesson or set as standalone homework; no teacher has to be present for a mission to work.

Individual B1–C1 Teen / Adult In-class or homework No prep

"Students tend to actually do these. The mission framing seems to help."

How to run a mission: hand out one card (or let the student pick), set a deadline — next lesson is usually enough — and have them report back in whatever form suits the class: two minutes of speaking, a short written note, or optionally a photo with a caption if the mission suits one. The report-back is where the language actually happens; the mission itself is just the reason to have something real to report.

01The Five-Minute Rewind

Think of something that happened to you in the last few days. Retell it backwards — starting from how it ended, and working back to how it started. Report back your backwards version.

02The Object Interrogation

Pick one real object within reach. Describe it in as much detail as you can — size, material, use, how it feels — without ever saying what it is. Report back your description, then reveal the object.

03The Category Break

List four ordinary things that belong to the same group (four foods, four jobs, four animals). Explain the rule that connects them — then add a fifth thing that almost fits, but breaks the rule, and explain exactly why it doesn't belong. Report back your group, your rule, and your odd one out.

04The Bad Advice Fix

Invent one piece of advice that sounds wrong, too formal, or slightly odd (e.g. "You should to eat before you are hungry"). Report back the bad version, then your corrected, natural-sounding version — and explain what you changed.

05Two Roads

Invent two realistic options for a small decision — two jobs, two apartments, two daily routines. Report back which one you'd choose and at least two specific reasons why, not just a preference.

06The Impossible Budget

Imagine you have a small, fixed amount of money (you choose the amount and the currency) and one goal to spend it on. Report back exactly how you'd spend it, item by item, and why.

07Yesterday, Reconstructed

Without writing anything down in advance, describe your entire day yesterday out loud, in order, for one full minute, in English. Report back how far you got before you had to pause and think.

08The Complaint Letter

Think of something mildly annoying that happened this week. Write four sentences complaining about it politely, as if to a company. Report back your four sentences.

09Somebody Else's Shoes

Think of a small real event from your week. Retell it again, but from another person's point of view — a shopkeeper, a bus driver, even a pet. Same event, different eyes. Report back your retelling.

10Set the Trap

Write three true statements and one false one — about yourself or an invented topic — worded so the false one isn't obvious. Report back your four statements, ready to give to a partner or classmate to guess.

11The Alibi

Pick a specific time yesterday (e.g. 6pm). Say exactly where you were and what you were doing — then add three convincing details that back it up (who else was there, what you could see, what happened right before or after). Make it airtight. Report back your alibi, details included.

12Photo, No Caption Needed

Take one photo of something in your daily life. Report back three full sentences describing it — what it is, where it is, why you chose it.

13The Upgrade

Describe something ordinary — your morning, your room, your walk to somewhere — in one plain, boring sentence. Now rewrite that same sentence to make it more interesting. Do it three times, each version better than the last. Report back all four sentences, plain to final.

14The Instruction Manual

Pick something you already know how to do well — a recipe, a game, a skill. Explain it step by step as if you're teaching someone who has genuinely never done it before. Report back your steps, in order.

15The Missing Middle

Here's a beginning: "I left the house in a hurry." And an end: "...and that's how I ended up laughing on the floor." Invent a believable middle that connects the two — or invent your own beginning and end, and fill the gap. Report back the full story, beginning to end.

16Tomorrow's Plan

Normally you'd say what you will do tomorrow. Instead, pretend tomorrow is already over and tell the story of what you "did" — using the past tense the whole way through. Report back your story of tomorrow.

Missions don't need to be assigned in order, and a student can repeat a favourite format with a new topic. The report-back is the part worth protecting time for — a mission without one is just a task nobody checks.

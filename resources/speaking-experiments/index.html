Speaking Experiments

Eight short speaking tasks built to produce genuine communication instead of scripted Q&A. Each one is designed to feel like an experiment, not a worksheet — something students actually have to work out in the moment, not recite.

~8 min each B1–C1 Teen / Adult Pairs or groups No prep

"I use these when a class is going through the motions. They tend to wake things up."

  1. 01 The Liar's Advantage

    One student tells a short story from their week — but changes exactly one detail to make it false. The partner asks follow-up questions and has one guess to name the false detail. The storyteller can't reveal it; the round ends on the guess, right or wrong.

    "Tell me about something that happened to you this week — but one part of it isn't true."

  2. 02 Three Words, No Repeats

    Give each pair three unrelated words (e.g. "umbrella," "deadline," "grandmother"). They have to build one short opinion or story that uses all three naturally — no repeating a word, no explaining why it's there. Forces real sentence construction instead of memorized phrases.

    "Use all three words in one connected story — don't just list them."

  3. 03 Defend the Indefensible

    Each student gets an absurd position to argue seriously for 90 seconds ("Mornings are better than evenings," "Cats are more useful than dogs at a workplace"). The partner isn't allowed to agree — only push back. Removes the pressure of having a "correct" real opinion.

    "You have 90 seconds to convince me — seriously — that [position]. I'm going to disagree with everything."

  4. 04 The Interruption Rule

    Normal small talk on any topic the pair chooses — except every 30–40 seconds, call out "switch," and the listener has to immediately summarize what the speaker just said before responding. Trains real listening, not just waiting for a turn to speak.

    Teacher calls "switch" every 30–40 seconds. Listener must summarize in one sentence before continuing.

  5. 05 Escalating Confession

    Each student shares three things about themselves, one at a time, getting slightly more surprising or personal each round (a preference, then a habit, then a genuine confession). The partner reacts genuinely rather than just nodding — this one relies on real curiosity, so it works best with groups who already know each other a little.

    "Tell me something small, then something a bit more surprising, then something true that might surprise me."

  6. 06 The One-Sided Phone Call

    One student "calls" the other about a minor everyday problem (a delayed order, a mix-up at a booking, a confusing bill) — but the listener can only respond, never see or hear anything written down. Good pressure-test for functional, real-time listening and negotiation language.

    "Call your partner about a problem with [an order / a booking / a bill]. They can only respond by voice — no notes."

  7. 07 Change My Mind

    Student A states a genuine (not absurd) opinion on something low-stakes — a film, a food, a daily habit. Student B's only job is to ask questions that might genuinely shift A's view, not argue against it. Runs 3–4 minutes. Works well as a cooldown after a louder activity.

    "Ask questions only — no arguing. See if you can get your partner to see it differently by the end."

  8. 08 The Missing Instruction

    One student explains how to do something ordinary (make coffee, tie a shoelace, set an alarm) to a partner who has to follow along literally — and the explainer deliberately leaves out one essential step. The listener has to notice the gap and ask the right question to find it.

    "Explain the process, but leave out one step on purpose. Your partner has to catch what's missing."

Run any of these cold, in any order — none depend on the others. Most work as written for B1–C1; for the lower end of that range, slow the pace and allow the listener to ask for repetition rather than simplifying the task itself.
